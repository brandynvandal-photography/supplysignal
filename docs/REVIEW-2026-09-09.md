## Where it stands

The honest assessment first, because it changes what "best on the market"
means here.

**The content is already the best in the category.** No consumer harm
reduction app pairs county-level alerts with adjacency, four strip types and
eight reagents with documented limits, an 841-pair interaction matrix, a
public corrections log, an *After* section written for both the survivor and
the witness, a supervision/drug-testing guide, and a policy page that names
the gaps in Good Samaritan laws state by state. DanceSafe has the testing
depth, Never Use Alone has the service, PsychonautWiki has the reference
breadth, the BC and Massachusetts programs have the lab data — nobody has the
combination, and nobody else publishes what they got wrong. Every dated
section is stamped August or September 2026. The privacy architecture is a
genuine differentiator, not a claim.

**The gap to "best" is not more content.** It is, in order:

1. **The first ten seconds on the two screens people land on.** The Alerts
   home and the county page both put the answer below the fold.
2. **The reader's own vocabulary.** Search knows 60 slang terms; the drug
   pages know none. The combination checker speaks TripSit's taxonomy, not
   the reader's.
3. **A live local signal.** The county page says "we have no public lab
   results for this area" for almost every county in the country. The alert
   feed is news and health-department RSS — announcements, which the
   README already recognizes are not the same as supply findings.
4. **Trust signals.** A health resource with no named editorial process is
   trusted less than a worse one with a face on it. The corrections log and
   the checked-dates are the strongest trust assets in the app and they are
   the least visible.
5. **Tools on the one screen where seconds matter.** SOS has no naloxone
   interval timer and no rescue-breath pacer, while Test already ships a
   15-second dip timer. The pattern exists; it is on the wrong tab.
6. **Spanish.** The chrome is translated; the 250 words that matter are not.

Sections below: fix-now bugs, wording, UI/UX, features, positioning and
roadmap, what I deliberately did not recommend, and what could be wrong.

## 2. Wording — body paragraphs

### The voice is right. Keep it.

Before edits: the register is the best thing about the writing. "You do not
have to say what they took — only that someone is not breathing." "Testing
finds red flags. It never gives a green light." "There is no single American
drug supply. There are at least four." "Nothing here is a judgment on how
you're living." "You do not owe anyone a transformation." "You were probably
angry at them while you were saving them." These are the sentences a
harm-reduction worker says out loud, and the app should be edited *toward*
them, not toward a house style.

The *After* page and the treatment-options section on *Support* are
finished writing. Do not touch them.

### Principles for the pass

1. **Cited language is never rewritten.** Anything with a source card, a
   quotation mark, or an "in their own words" is off limits. TripSit notes,
   SAMHSA myth corrections, hotline self-descriptions, RaDAR sentences.
2. **Instruction before explanation.** On SOS and Test the first sentence of
   every block is the thing to do; the *why* follows. Several Test blocks
   invert this (below).
3. **One claim, one number, one source, in that order.** Where a paragraph
   carries three numbers, the reader at 3am gets none of them.
4. **Say "most" only with a source on the same screen.** EVIDENCE.md already
   requires this; a few editorial lines drifted.
5. **Prefer the contracted form.** "Don't", "isn't", "you're". The kindness
   lines and the *After* page use them; SOS and Test mostly don't, which
   reads as a register change between tabs.

### Specific edits (editorial text only)

**Test — strip reading block, BTNX row.**
> "It missed 52 of the fentanyl analogues in the lab screen. That is more misses than the other brand. It did find more compounds overall at high amounts: 173 against 149."

Three numbers, no source in the block, no instruction. Rewrite:
> "No strip catches every fentanyl-like drug. In a lab screen this brand missed 52 of them — more than the other brand, though it caught more compounds overall. Treat a negative as 'nothing found', not 'nothing there'."

Put the source card directly under it.

**Test — brands not listed.**
> "Wondfo, DetectaChem and MD-Bio are not listed as we have not verified instructions at this time."

> "Wondfo, DetectaChem and MD-Bio strips aren't here yet — we haven't verified their instructions."

**Test — "Whatever the test says".**
> "Start low and go slow. Tolerance and how long you've been away from a substance is a factor."

Grammar (plural subject, singular verb) and vague. Rewrite:
> "Start low and go slow. What you can handle depends on your tolerance — and on how long it's been since you last used."

> "Using alone is what turns most overdoses into accidents — not the dose, the fact that nobody was there to notice."

The metaphor is muddled and "most" is unsourced here. Rewrite, and attach the
SUDORS bystander figure:
> "Most people who die of an overdose were alone. It's usually not the dose that kills — it's that nobody was there to notice. If there's no one around, Never Use Alone answers 24/7 at 1-800-484-3731 and sends help if you stop responding."

### Consistency

- **Naloxone / Narcan.** The app says naloxone throughout and Narcan only in
  slang search — correct. Keep it; add "Narcan" as an inline parenthetical
  once on SOS ("naloxone — Narcan is one brand") because that is the word
  people arrive with.
- **Overdose / poisoning.** Consistently "overdose". Good. Some
  harm-reduction orgs now prefer "drug poisoning" for contaminated supply;
  don't switch — the reader's word is overdose.
- **Tranq.** Introduced as 'xylazine ("tranq")' on Drugs and as "tranq" alone

## 3. UI/UX

Verified at 390×844 (dark and light), 1024×800, with computed styles where
it mattered. Tap targets are honored: the only sub-44px controls on the
whole surface are "Tell us" (50×26) and the footer mail link. Contrast tokens

### 3.1 Alerts home — five blocks before the first alert

At phone size the order is: welcome card, H1, "Not sure where to start"
card, List/Map toggle, search + Near me, "National alerts" heading — and
the fold. The reader's primary action (search or Near me) is the fifth
element. Reorder:

1. Search + Near me (the app's primary interaction, per README).
2. One-line welcome *under* the search, not above it: "Free, no account.
   Nothing you look up here leaves this device."
3. "Not sure where to start" — keep; it is the right card for the reader who
   cannot name what they need.
4. List/Map toggle attached to the results, not floating above them.

Drop the welcome card's headline "You're in the right place." on repeat
visits? No — there are no repeat visits to detect, by design. Just make it
one line.

### 3.2 County page — answer first, map second

Current order: H1, FIPS, search, map, legend, two caveat pills, three hint
pills, window chips, **then** the status callout. Reader's question: *is
there anything here?* Proposed:

1. H1 + one status line with a glyph and a word: "◉ Nothing published for
   Davidson County in 90 days · scanned 2026-09-09" or "● Not yet scanned".
2. Alerts here, then bordering counties **collapsed into one row** when all
   are empty — "6 bordering counties, nothing published in any of them ›" —
   rather than six rows of "none". Expanded automatically if any has an
   alert.
3. Overdose deaths block (already excellent).
4. Recent drug checking.
5. Map, collapsed on phones behind "Show the map", open on tablet and up.

The 3D map is beautiful and it is the most expensive thing on the page for
the 2GB-Android persona; on a phone it should be opt-in.

### 3.3 Jump strips

Fix-now #8. The strips are the page's table of contents and they are
invisible past the third item. Two rows under 480px costs ~40px and makes
every section reachable; measure both.

### 3.4 Combination checker — replace the native selects

Two `<select>`s with 31 TripSit categories in alphabetical order: 2C-T-x,
2C-x, 5-MeO-xxT, Alcohol, Amphetamines, AMT… A reader who took "a Xanax and
some fent" has to know that means Benzodiazepines + Opioids and scroll past
four research-chemical families to find either. On iOS the select is a wheel.

Use the pattern the app already has (`.pick__row` / `.mixslot`, per the
mirror-existing-formatting rule): a searchable row, common-first ordering
(Opioids, Benzos, Alcohol, Cocaine, Meth, Xylazine…), slang matched through
the same table as search ("xans" → Benzodiazepines, "blues" → Opioids), and
the four "not in the chart" pairs (xylazine, gabapentin, medetomidine,
nitazenes) selectable rather than listed separately — selecting one shows
the existing note. Then the "Categories, not brands" sentence can go.

### 3.5 Combination result — one headline, not two

The pair name + DANGEROUS badge renders twice (once with TripSit's generic
text, once with the specific note). One badge, the specific note first, the
generic sentence as the collapsed "why" — TripSit's text intact.

### 3.6 Drug page — the molecule is decoration

Fentanyl at 390: title, twelve pharma aliases, a 2D structure that fills a
third of the screen, PubChem attribution, class chips — then the first
sentence a reader needs. Move the structure below "Coming down" or behind a
disclosure. Reorder routes by relevance to the street supply: for fentanyl
that is smoked first, then insufflated, then injected; transdermal last. Where
PsychonautWiki has no smoked-route timing, say so in the table rather than
omitting the route — "Smoked: onset within a minute or two; no published
duration range" (source needed; see Features §4.5).

### 3.7 Hotlines — a structured dispatch row

Wording §2 covers the copy; the UI is a labeled row with a glyph, the same
component as the severity badge, so a reader can scan twelve lines for the
one fact they care about.

### 3.8 SOS — timers on the highest-stakes screen

Test has a 15-second dip timer built on `clock.js`. SOS has the two places a
timer matters most and has neither:

- **"No better after 2–3 minutes? Give another dose"** → a "Start 3 minutes"
  button in the step, counting down on screen, ending in "Still no better?
  Give the second dose" — not a sound (someone else may be in the room).
- **"One breath every 5 seconds"** → a visual pulse, full-width, no audio by
  default, optional haptic. The number is impossible to keep under stress
  and trivial to show.

Both are local, offline, and zero-cost to privacy. Reuse `clock.js`.

### 3.9 Type scale

Fix-now #11. Six steps: `--fs-xs .74 / --fs-sm .82 / --fs-body 1 /
--fs-lg 1.12 / --fs-xl 1.3 / --fs-h1 1.6rem`. Everything else is one of
those. The measure (68ch), line height (1.65) and 17px base are right and
should not move.

### 3.10 Early-access banner

27px of permanent chrome that hedges every screen equally. Wording §2 gives
the alternative. If retained, it should at least not render on Test's guided
walkthrough or the county page's status line — the two places a reader is
acting on the text in front of them.

## 5. Positioning — what "best on the market" means here

**The category has no incumbent app.** Verified in the landscape pass
(`REVIEW-2026-09-09-research.md`): DanceSafe is a website and a nonprofit
with no app and leadership churn; Never Use Alone and SafeSpot are phone
numbers; Brave's free spotting app was retired in January 2025 and no live
spotter app remains on either store; Lifeguard's corporate site is down;
PsychonautWiki's app is a paywalled journal; TripSit's is Android-only and
possibly stale; the drug-checking portals are regional and none has a feed;
KnowDrugs (200,000 downloads) is the closest analog and it is German. The
2025–26 US entrants — Overdose Partner (offline naloxone steps), ReviveAR
(Arkansas; English/Spanish/Marshallese), Naxos OD, Harm Reduction Sisters
(chat) — each do one thing. Nobody ships the whole loop — *what's around
here → how to test it → what mixes badly → what to do if it goes wrong →
what happens after → where to get help* — and nobody ships it offline with
no account. Nightlight already does. So "best" is not a feature race; it is
being the one the harm-reduction field points people at.

**What the evidence lets you claim, and what it doesn't.** The evidence pass
(same appendix) is worth reading in full; the parts that change decisions:

- *Drug checking changes dosing, not (measurably) overdoses.* Positive or
  unexpected results reliably change behavior — Gelberg 2025: used less,
  OR 4.37; Tobias 2026: aOR 2.24 for a risk-reduction practice — but the
  only prospective cohort with an overdose endpoint (Vickers-Smith, JAMA
  Netw Open 2025) found no difference. Keep the Test tab's framing exactly
  as it is: "test it, then go slow, not alone, naloxone nearby." Never
  claim outcomes.
- *People prefer a human on the phone to a timer app* (Rioux 2024), the
  number-one barrier to using any spotting service is police attendance
  (Seo 2026, n=971), and only 1 in 10 US treatment clients had heard of the
  hotlines at all (Gicquelais 2025). Awareness is the job. Nightlight
  already puts NUA on every surface; §4.6b adds SafeSpot.
- *Alerts reach people through peers and social media*, and 67% of the
  alert-aware changed behavior in the one survey that asked (Daowd 2023).
  The per-county RSS feeds and the printable county sheet are the right
  channels; alert fatigue has never been studied in this population, which
  is one more reason to default the home screen to 90 days (Fix-now #6).
- *Naloxone locators have never been evaluated* but are low-risk, and
  removing registration and limits measurably increases uptake (Herrera
  2026). §4.7 stands.
- *Sensors and AI have no place here.* The one FDA-authorized overdose
  wearable has no published effectiveness data; the UW sonar app was
  absorbed by Google without a product; users themselves prefer a human
  alert over automation because of false positives (Roth 2025).

**The 2026 federal context is a differentiator.** Executive Order 14321
(July 2025) bars HHS discretionary grants from funding "harm reduction"
efforts; April 2026 SAMHSA guidance bars federal grant money for test
strips, syringes and "specialized overdose prevention hotlines"; grantees
were told in January 2026 not to promote the "never use alone" message.
Funded programs are now constrained in what they can say and hand out.
Nightlight takes no money from anyone. Say so on About, and make the Policy
page carry these three facts with dates and sources — it is exactly the
"who funds this" question that page already asks.

That is won on three things:

1. **Trust you can see.** Today the About page says *what* it is and not
   *who*. A health resource is trusted by the people who vouch for it. The
   assets already exist and are hidden: a public corrections log, an
   evidence standard with a dated audit, per-section checked dates, a
   privacy architecture enforced by tests. Put them on the surface: a
   "How we check" section on About with the audit table from EVIDENCE.md,
   the corrections log one tap from every "Checked" stamp, and a named
   maintainer or organization. Then ask two or three harm-reduction
   organizations to review specific pages and say so ("SOS steps reviewed
   by …, August 2026"). Nashville has SSPs and a health department drug
   overdose program; that is the first conversation.

2. **A local signal nobody else has** (§4.4). Lab and ME data by county is
   the product. News alerts got the app to here; they will not get it to
   "best".

3. **Distribution through the field, not the store.** The reader the brief
   describes does not browse the App Store for harm reduction. They are
   handed things: a strip, a naloxone kit, a card. §4.11 makes the app the
   card. The public per-county RSS feeds are a second channel — health
   departments and outreach orgs can subscribe today; tell them.

**Measuring it without analytics.** The brief forbids the instruments most
products use, so measure the artifact, not the reader: time-to-first-true-
thing on a throttled device (stopwatch, four screens, monthly); number of
counties with a lab result younger than 60 days; number of sections with a
"Checked" stamp older than 90 days (should be zero); link-rot count from
`check:links`; test count; and a quarterly note from three outreach workers
who use it. Publish those numbers on About. That *is* the trust surface.

## 6. What I deliberately did not recommend, and why

- **An AI assistant / chatbot.** Every answer would be an unverified
  sentence about a drug, generated at the moment of highest stakes. The
  brand is that no sentence here is unverified. Curated intents in search
  already do the "I can't name what I need" job, on device.
- **Accounts, saved counties, "my kit" trackers, push alerts for a county.**
  All remembered state is a subpoena target and a lock-screen leak. The
  per-county RSS feed is the opt-in channel that leaves nothing on the
  phone.
- **Crowd-sourced "I saw this in my bag" reports.** Unverifiable, and one
  false report is worse than none. Point readers to the checking programs
  that will actually test it (§4.4, §4.7).
- **Virtual spotting inside the app.** Brave and NORS do this with staffed
  servers and consent frameworks. An app-local timer that "calls for help"
  cannot call anyone; a notification would out the reader; a half-built
  spotting feature is a promise the app cannot keep. Never Use Alone is the
  right answer and it is already on every surface.
- **Softening the 988 dispatch caveat.** It is long, it is the first thing
  under "Someone to talk to now", and it is the truth the reader needs to
  choose a line. Structure it (§3.7); do not shorten it.
- **A "calmer" SOS.** It is calm. The critical callout, the 911 button and
  the six steps are the best-executed screen in the app.

## 7. What could still be wrong

- The reordering proposals (§3.1, §3.2) are judgments from the persona test,
  not from watching readers. Three sessions with outreach workers would
  settle them faster than any argument here.
- The 90-day national default (Fix-now #6) trades a stale CRITICAL for a
  possibly empty list. The empty state already says the right thing; verify
  it renders on the national list, not only per county.
- The two-row jump strip may cost more on Learn (8 chips, long labels) than
  it saves; measure at 375 and 390 before committing.
- Street names are regional and they drift; a curated list needs an owner
  and a re-check date like everything else.
- The "abuse" remap (Fix-now #4) changes a derived PsychonautWiki field.
  CC BY-SA permits adaptation with attribution; the attribution line should
  say "adapted" rather than "from".
- Hidden-pane rendering in the audit browser produced one black screenshot
  after a scripted jump on Test; I could not reproduce it and it is
  consistent with the known hidden-tab paint throttle, not the site. Worth a
  look on a real device: tap "Reagents" on Test and confirm the scroll lands
  on the section.
- Two research tracks — what people who use drugs and outreach workers say
  is missing from existing tools, and the 2025–26 App Store / Google Play
  policy and state-law landscape — were still running when this was
  written. They land as an addendum to the research appendix; nothing above
  depends on them.
- The research appendix was produced by research agents under a search
  budget that ran out partway; each report names what it could not verify.
  Treat its numbers as leads with citations, not as checked facts, until
  you have opened the source.
